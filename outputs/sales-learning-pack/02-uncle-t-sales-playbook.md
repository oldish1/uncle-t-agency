# The Uncle T Sales Playbook

How to sell Lexi and websites to Cape Town salon owners, using what the best salespeople in the world teach. Written as a NotebookLM source, so the AI can connect the YouTube lessons to Theo's real business.

## Who Theo is selling to

Theo runs Uncle T Agency in Cape Town. He sells two things to small businesses, mostly hair salons:

- **Lexi**, a WhatsApp booking assistant that lives inside the salon's own WhatsApp Business number. It greets clients, shows services, books straight into the calendar without double-booking, and sends reminders so fewer people no-show. R1,499 once-off setup, R499 a month. Live in 4 days.
- **Websites**, a mobile-first site for R1,499 once-off, hosting and domain inside the R499 a month. Competitors like Telecloud charge R2,000 to R11,000 and bill hosting separately.

His proof is CHALES Hair Boutique, Chante's salon, which already runs Lexi. His warm leads include Zanzibar Hair and Beauty, Sistergirl, Reeva Hair, JEM Hair and Beauty Studio, and a tattoo artist at No Love Lost.

The typical buyer is a salon owner, often a woman, who is also the main stylist. She answers WhatsApp between clients with wet hands, keeps a paper diary or her memory, and loses bookings at night because she's asleep or exhausted. She's been burned before by people selling "marketing" and "tech." She doesn't trust salesmen. She does trust people who understand her day.

## The big mistake Theo has been making

Theo builds excellent automations. When he talks to a prospect, the natural thing is to explain what he built: the AI, the calendar sync, the reminders. Every teacher in this pack says the same thing about that: **nobody buys the product. They buy the end of a problem they feel.**

If the owner hasn't said her problem out loud, in her own words, she won't buy, however good Lexi is. Theo's job is to get her to say it. The talking ratio should be about 70% her, 30% Theo.

## Lesson 1: Make them comfortable first (Chris Voss)

Chris Voss was the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator. His main idea is **tactical empathy**: show the other person you understand how they feel before you ask for anything. People only open up when they feel safe and understood.

Voss's tools, as Theo would use them at a salon counter:

- **Mirroring.** Repeat the last one to three words she said, as a gentle question. She says "Saturdays are crazy." Theo says "Crazy?" and goes quiet. She'll explain, usually with the real pain inside it.
- **Labelling.** Name the feeling you can see. Start with "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…". Never "I hear you saying" (that puts the focus on Theo). "It sounds like you're the receptionist, the stylist and the owner all at once." When a label lands, people say "Exactly," and the guard drops.
- **The accusation audit.** Say the bad thing she's thinking before she does. "You probably get people walking in here every week trying to sell you something, and I'm sure most of it's rubbish." It lowers resistance because it shows Theo isn't blind to how he looks.
- **Aim for "That's right," not "You're right."** "You're right" means "please go away." "That's right" means she feels understood. Summarise her situation back to her until she says it.
- **No-oriented questions.** People feel safer saying no. Instead of "Do you have two minutes?" ask "Is now a bad time?" Instead of "Would you like to see it?" ask "Would it be crazy to look at something on my phone for two minutes?"
- **The late-night FM DJ voice.** Slow, calm, low, warm. Speed and excitement read as pushy.

## Lesson 2: Let her tell you the pain (Miner, SPIN, Sandler, Keenan)

This is the part that turns "I can't sell" into "she asked me how to sign up."

**Jeremy Miner (NEPQ)** teaches that people believe what they say, not what you say. So ask questions that make her discover the problem herself. Neutral tone, curious, never leading.

**Neil Rackham (SPIN Selling)** studied 35,000 sales calls. The winners asked four kinds of questions in order:

1. **Situation**: how things work now. Keep these few, they bore people.
2. **Problem**: where it goes wrong.
3. **Implication**: what that problem costs, and what it causes. This is where big sales are won.
4. **Need-payoff**: get her to say what fixing it would be worth.

**Sandler's pain funnel** goes from surface to emotion: tell me more, can you give me an example, how long has that been going on, what have you tried, how did that work, how much has it cost you, how do you feel about that, have you given up trying?

**Keenan (Gap Selling)** says the sale lives in the gap between her current state and the future she wants. The bigger and clearer the gap, the easier the sale. Price only feels expensive when the gap is small.

### Salon pain questions, in order

Situation (two or three, max):
- "How do most of your clients book with you at the moment?"
- "Who answers WhatsApp while you're busy with someone in the chair?"

Problem:
- "What happens to messages that come in while you're mid-colour?"
- "How often do you get no-shows in a normal week?"
- "Has a double-booking ever happened? What did that look like?"

Implication (go slow here, this is where the money is):
- "When someone messages at 9pm for a Saturday slot and you only see it the next morning, what do you think they've done by then?"
- "If you lose two bookings a week like that, at around R400 each, what does that come to over a month?" (Let HER do the maths. R3,200 a month. Lexi is R499.)
- "What does it do to your evenings, answering WhatsApps in bed?"
- "How does your family feel about the phone at dinner?"

Need-payoff:
- "If every message got answered in seconds, even at midnight, what would that mean for you?"
- "What would you do with the hour a day you spend replying to messages?"

**Consequence questions** (Miner): "What happens if nothing changes for the next year?" Ask it softly, then stay quiet.

**Silence is a tool.** After a big question, count to five in your head. Whoever fills the silence gives information. Let it be her.

## Lesson 3: Closing without being pushy (Hormozi, Belfort)

**Alex Hormozi's CLOSER framework**, built from thousands of closes:

- **C**larify why she's talking to you. "What made you give me two minutes today?"
- **L**abel the problem. "So it sounds like the real issue is you can't be with clients and on WhatsApp at the same time."
- **O**verview past pain. "What have you tried before? Did the booking app work out? Why not?" (This shows her the old fixes failed, so Lexi isn't one more of them.)
- **S**ell the vacation, not the flight. Don't explain how Lexi works. Describe her life after: "You finish your last client, you check your phone, and tomorrow's diary is full and confirmed. You didn't type one message."
- **E**xplain away concerns. Handle the objections (Lesson 4).
- **R**einforce the decision. After she says yes, tell her she made a good call, and what happens next. "Perfect, I'll come Thursday, set it up, and by Monday it's live."

Hormozi on price: **the more someone pays, the more they value it.** Don't apologise for R499. Say it once, clearly, and then stop talking.

The **assumed close**: once she's agreed the pain is real and the fix is worth it, don't ask "so do you want it?" Ask a logistics question: "Would you want it live before the December rush, or after?"

**Downselling**: if she can't do the full offer, have a smaller yes ready, like a website alone, or a two-week trial at a founding-client rate. A small yes now beats a no.

**Jordan Belfort's Straight Line**: every sale is a straight line from open to close. The prospect will try to pull you off the line with tangents. Be friendly, then steer back. She needs to be certain about three things before she buys: **the product, you, and the company.** Rate each one in your head from 1 to 10. CHALES is your proof for the product. Being local, calm and honest is your proof for you. A live demo on your phone does more than any explanation.

## Lesson 4: Objections (Miner, Cardone, Ziglar)

An objection is usually a question, or fear in disguise. Never argue. Get curious.

**Miner's method**: clarify first. "Too expensive compared to what?" or "When you say you need to think about it, what's the part you want to think about?" The real objection is usually the second thing she says.

**Zig Ziglar, cost versus price**: price is what she pays once. Cost is what she loses by not having it. A R499 system that saves two bookings a month pays for itself before the second week.

The common ones and how to answer them:

- **"It's too expensive."** "Fair. What do you think one missed booking costs you?" Then, "So if it saves one booking a week, it's paid for itself by Tuesday?"
- **"I need to think about it."** "Of course. What's the part you're unsure about? Is it the money, whether it'll actually work, or something else?"
- **"I'll ask my partner."** "Makes sense. What do you think they'll ask? Let's make sure you have the answer."
- **"My clients like talking to me, not a robot."** "That's right, and you'll still talk to them in the chair. This just answers the 9pm 'are you open Saturday?' message so they don't go somewhere else. Want to see what it says?"
- **"I already use Booksy / Fresha / a diary."** "How's that working? Do clients actually use the link, or do they still WhatsApp you?" (Usually they WhatsApp anyway.)
- **"I tried something before and it didn't work."** "What happened?" Listen fully. Then explain the one thing that's different, and offer to show CHALES.
- **"Not now, maybe after December."** "What changes after December?" Then, "December's the busiest month for missed messages. Would it be crazy to have it running before then?"

**Grant Cardone**: never lower the price because of an objection. Add value or remove the risk instead (a founding-client deal, a testimonial swap, a 30-day guarantee if you're comfortable offering one).

## Lesson 5: Walking in cold

- Read the room before you speak. Paper diary on the counter, phone buzzing, owner cutting hair and answering WhatsApp? That's your opening line.
- Never pitch over a client in the chair. Leave a card, ask when to come back, then actually come back.
- Say who you are, that you're local, and why you're there, in one breath. No "how are you today?" warm-up.
- **Jeb Blount**: the pipeline fills from volume. Ten real conversations a week beat one perfect pitch. Most no's mean "not today."
- **The fear of rejection** shrinks when you treat each visit as research, not a test. Your goal walking in is to learn her pain, not to close.

## Lesson 6: Selling AI to people who don't care about AI

The AI-agency teachers (Nate Herk, Jason Wardrop and others) agree on this:

- Never say "AI," "chatbot," "automation" or "technology" unless she does first. Say "a receptionist who never sleeps and never double-books."
- Show, don't explain. Message the demo number live in front of her. Speed sells it.
- Sell outcomes in Rand. "More bookings, fewer no-shows, your evenings back."
- Proof beats promises. "Chante at CHALES runs it. Want me to show you a real conversation from her salon?"
- Start small and expand. One working system, then the website, then reviews and reactivation later.

## The 2-minute salon conversation, start to finish

1. **Open:** "Hi, I'm Theo, I'm local, I help salons around here stop losing bookings on WhatsApp. Is now a terrible time?"
2. **Accusation audit:** "You probably get people in here every week selling stuff, so I'll be quick."
3. **Pain questions:** missed messages, late-night messages, no-shows. Mirror and label her answers.
4. **Implication:** let her work out what it costs per month.
5. **Need-payoff:** "What would it mean if every message got answered in seconds?"
6. **Demo:** live on your phone, under two minutes.
7. **Price, said once:** "R1,499 to set up, then R499 a month."
8. **Silence.** Let her respond.
9. **Handle the objection** with a clarifying question.
10. **Assumed close:** "Thursday or Friday to set it up?"
11. **Reinforce:** "Good call. Here's what happens next."

## Daily practice

- Practise one Voss tool per day on everyone: the barista, family, a supplier. Mirroring works anywhere.
- Before every visit, write down three pain questions you'll ask.
- After every visit, note what she said in her own words. Those phrases become your next marketing message.
- Role-play with NotebookLM or Claude: ask it to play a sceptical salon owner in Bellville and push back hard.

## Lesson 7: Dan Martell's 4-step process (Theo's favourite)

Source: "If you only watch one sales video, make it this one" (youtube.com/watch?v=nsFKkeQg-V0). Dan was a coder who built great tech, had two companies fail, and nobody paid him until he learned to sell.

- Selling is serving: you help someone make a decision. People love to buy, they hate being sold to.
- Ask yourself "Can I actually help this person?" If yes, it's your job to help them decide.
- Pros follow a process. "If you don't have a process for selling, your prospect will show you their process for not buying."

1. **Connect.** Doctor frame. Ask "Why me?" and "Why now?"
2. **Diagnose.** Stretch the gap: the dream a year from now, where she is today, and "What have you tried before?" Write down every roadblock.
3. **Prescribe.** Repeat her roadblocks back, ask "Where do you want to go from here?", pitch only what fixes those roadblocks. Clear the 3 obstacles (who decides, budget, time) before the price. Say the price, add one more benefit, then ask.
4. **Commit.** A yes isn't a sale until there's a deposit. Never "I'll send a proposal."

After every visit, review: did I connect, find real pain, keep it simple, ask for the money with confidence?
